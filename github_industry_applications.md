# Industry Applications: AI Cost Attribution Framework

This document provides sector-specific implementation guidance for applying the AI Cost Attribution Framework across four major U.S. industries. Each sector faces a version of the same structural problem — costs generated by complex operational activity attributed using proxy metrics that do not reflect actual consumption — and the same methodology applies with industry-specific driver adaptations.

---

## Pharmaceutical Manufacturing

### The Problem

U.S. pharmaceutical manufacturers operate shared production facilities — equipment, utilities, quality control infrastructure, regulatory compliance systems — across multiple drug product lines. Traditional cost allocation distributes these shared facility costs using headcount or revenue percentage, both of which bear little relationship to the actual production activity driving costs.

The result: high-volume, low-margin drugs absorb costs disproportionate to their actual resource consumption. Novel, high-margin drugs appear more profitable than they are. Investment decisions about which treatments to develop are made on distorted product P&L foundations.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics documented a 2.4% annual productivity decline in U.S. pharmaceutical and medicine manufacturing between 2014 and 2024 — one of the worst among all U.S. manufacturing industries. Inaccurate cost attribution is a contributing factor: organizations cannot optimize what they cannot accurately measure.

### Driver Mapping for Pharmaceutical Manufacturing

| Cost Pool | Primary Driver | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Production equipment depreciation | Batch volume | Equipment cost scales with production runs |
| Facility utilities (power, water, HVAC) | Equipment utilization hours | Utility consumption directly proportional to equipment runtime |
| Quality control and testing | Batch count | Each batch requires QC testing regardless of size |
| Regulatory compliance infrastructure | Number of active SKUs | Compliance costs driven by product count |
| Raw material handling | Material consumption weight | Supply chain costs scale with material volume |
| Engineering and maintenance | Equipment utilization hours | Maintenance requirements scale with runtime |
| General production overhead | Headcount by production line | No consumption metric available |

### Implementation Steps

**Step 1: Define batch-level driver data collection**

For each drug product line, collect monthly:
```
- Number of batches produced
- Batch size (units per batch)
- Equipment utilization hours per batch
- Material consumption (kg or units)
- QC tests conducted
- Active SKU count
```

**Step 2: Map shared facilities to production drivers**

```python
pharma_driver_mapping = {
    'production_equipment_depreciation': 'batch_volume',
    'facility_utilities': 'equipment_utilization_hours',
    'qc_and_testing': 'batch_count',
    'regulatory_compliance': 'active_sku_count',
    'raw_material_handling': 'material_consumption_kg',
    'engineering_maintenance': 'equipment_utilization_hours',
    'general_overhead': 'headcount'
}
```

**Step 3: Build the allocation model**

```python
from src.cost_attribution import CostAttributionModel

model = CostAttributionModel(
    cost_pools=pharma_cost_pools,
    driver_data=pharma_driver_data,
    period='2026-Q2',
    industry='pharmaceutical_manufacturing'
)

results = model.run_attribution()
```

### Validation Considerations

- **Regulatory auditability**: Pharmaceutical cost allocation may be subject to FDA audit. Document every driver mapping decision and maintain version history of methodology changes.
- **Batch yield variation**: Adjust batch volume driver for yield rate — a batch with 95% yield should not receive the same allocation as a batch with 70% yield for quality-related costs.
- **Campaign production**: For drug products manufactured in production campaigns, aggregate campaign-level driver data before calculating cost shares.

### Expected Impact

Organizations implementing usage-based attribution in pharmaceutical manufacturing typically find:
- 15-40% reallocation of shared facility costs across product lines
- Material improvement in product-level profitability accuracy
- Better-informed decisions about production line investment and capacity planning

## Healthcare Systems

### The Problem

Hospital systems and healthcare networks operate shared clinical infrastructure — imaging equipment, surgical suites, laboratory systems, diagnostic technology, administrative systems — across multiple clinical departments and service lines. Traditional overhead allocation distributes these shared costs by patient census, bed count, or revenue — none of which reflect how clinical resources are actually consumed.

The consequences extend beyond financial reporting. When clinical departments receive inaccurate cost allocations, hospital leadership cannot make reliable decisions about which service lines to expand, which departments are genuinely profitable, or how to price services sustainably. The affordability challenges facing U.S. healthcare systems are compounded when capital allocation decisions are made on distorted financial foundations.

### Driver Mapping for Healthcare Systems

| Cost Pool | Primary Driver | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Imaging equipment (MRI, CT, X-ray) | Procedure volumes by modality | Equipment cost scales with scan count and complexity |
| Surgical suite infrastructure | OR hours utilized | Suite costs scale with actual utilization time |
| Laboratory systems | Test volumes | Lab costs scale with the number of tests processed |
| ICU and high-dependency infrastructure | Patient acuity score × bed-days | High-acuity patients consume disproportionate resources |
| Emergency department infrastructure | ED visits | ED costs scale with patient visit volume |
| Administrative and EHR systems | Patient encounters | Administrative systems serve patient volume |
| Pharmacy infrastructure | Prescription volume | Pharmacy costs scale with prescription count |
| General facility overhead | Bed-days | Last-resort proxy for unmeasured consumption |

### Implementation Steps

**Step 1: Extract clinical activity data by department**

```
For each clinical department, collect monthly:
- Patient encounters by type (inpatient, outpatient, ED, surgical)
- Procedure volumes by modality (imaging scans, lab tests, surgical procedures)
- OR hours utilized
- Patient acuity scores (CMI — Case Mix Index)
- Bed-days by patient type
- Prescription volumes
```

**Step 2: Map shared infrastructure to clinical drivers**

```python
healthcare_driver_mapping = {
    'imaging_equipment': 'procedure_volumes_by_modality',
    'surgical_suite': 'or_hours_utilized',
    'laboratory_systems': 'test_volumes',
    'icu_infrastructure': 'acuity_weighted_bed_days',
    'ed_infrastructure': 'ed_visits',
    'admin_ehr_systems': 'patient_encounters',
    'pharmacy': 'prescription_volume',
    'general_facility': 'bed_days'
}
```

**Step 3: Calculate acuity-weighted bed-days**

Simple bed-day counts treat all patients equally. Acuity-weighted bed-days adjust for the actual resource intensity of each patient:

```python
def calculate_acuity_weighted_bed_days(bed_days, case_mix_index):
    """
    Acuity-weighted bed-days = bed-days × CMI
    Higher CMI = higher resource consumption per day
    """
    return bed_days * case_mix_index
```

### HIPAA Considerations

Cost attribution models for healthcare use aggregate departmental data — not patient-level data. The driver metrics (procedure volumes, encounter counts, bed-days by department) are operational statistics, not protected health information.

However:
- Do not use patient-level data as attribution drivers
- Aggregate all metrics to the department level before use in the model
- Consult your compliance team before connecting clinical data systems to financial attribution models

### Expected Impact

Healthcare systems implementing usage-based attribution typically find:
- Surgical service lines and high-acuity departments receiving more accurate (typically higher) cost allocations
- Administrative and low-acuity service lines receiving lower allocations than under bed-count methods
- More reliable service line profitability analysis for strategic planning

## Logistics and Supply Chain

### The Problem

Logistics organizations operate shared fleet infrastructure, distribution facilities, and technology systems across multiple service tiers — same-day delivery, standard delivery, freight, specialized handling — and customer segments. Flat-rate or revenue-percentage allocation distributes these shared costs without regard to the actual cost drivers: shipment characteristics, route complexity, handling requirements, and delivery speed.

The result: high-complexity, low-value shipments appear more profitable than they are. Premium service tiers cross-subsidize standard tiers. Pricing decisions are made without accurate visibility into the cost of serving different customer segments and shipment types.

### Driver Mapping for Logistics and Supply Chain

| Cost Pool | Primary Driver | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Fleet fuel and maintenance | Route miles driven | Direct proportional relationship |
| Vehicle depreciation | Route miles driven | Depreciation scales with utilization |
| Driver labor | Delivery hours | Labor cost scales with time on route |
| Warehouse handling | Handling events (picks, packs, loads) | Labor cost scales with touches |
| Warehouse space | Cubic feet occupied by SKU | Space cost scales with inventory footprint |
| Last-mile delivery infrastructure | Package count by delivery zone | Last-mile cost scales with stop count and geography |
| Freight infrastructure | Weight × distance (cwt-miles) | Standard freight cost unit |
| Technology and tracking systems | Shipment count | Technology scales with transaction volume |

### Implementation Steps

**Step 1: Define shipment-level attributes**

```
For each service tier or customer segment, collect monthly:
- Shipment count
- Total route miles
- Delivery hours
- Package weight and dimensions (for cube and weight calculations)
- Handling events (warehouse picks, packs, quality checks, loads)
- Cubic feet of warehouse space occupied
- Delivery zone distribution
```

**Step 2: Build the cost-per-unit framework**

```python
def calculate_logistics_cost_drivers(shipments_df):
    """
    Calculate primary logistics cost drivers by service tier
    """
    return {
        'route_miles': shipments_df.groupby('service_tier')['route_miles'].sum(),
        'delivery_hours': shipments_df.groupby('service_tier')['delivery_hours'].sum(),
        'handling_events': shipments_df.groupby('service_tier')['handling_events'].sum(),
        'shipment_count': shipments_df.groupby('service_tier')['shipment_count'].sum(),
        'cwt_miles': (shipments_df['weight_cwt'] * shipments_df['route_miles']).groupby(shipments_df['service_tier']).sum()
    }
```

**Step 3: Identify cross-subsidization**

After implementing usage-based attribution, compare allocated costs to revenue by service tier:

```python
def identify_cross_subsidization(allocated_costs, revenue_by_tier):
    """
    Identify service tiers where costs exceed revenue
    or where legacy allocation was significantly distorting margins
    """
    margin_analysis = revenue_by_tier - allocated_costs
    legacy_margin_analysis = revenue_by_tier - legacy_allocated_costs

    delta = margin_analysis - legacy_margin_analysis
    return delta.sort_values()  # Most impacted tiers first
```

### Expected Impact

Logistics organizations implementing usage-based attribution typically find:
- Premium, time-sensitive service tiers receiving higher cost allocations (reflecting their true infrastructure intensity)
- Standard service tiers receiving lower allocations (reflecting less infrastructure consumption)
- Cross-subsidization patterns that inform pricing strategy and service tier design decisions

## Financial Services

### The Problem

Financial services firms — banks, fintech companies, insurance providers, investment platforms — operate shared technology infrastructure across multiple product lines: retail banking, commercial banking, wealth management, payments processing, risk systems. Revenue-percentage allocation distributes technology costs without any relationship to how different products actually consume shared systems.

As AI-powered financial services scale, this problem intensifies. GPU compute costs, model inference infrastructure, data processing systems, and real-time risk calculation platforms are consumed at very different rates by different product lines. Attributing these costs by revenue percentage produces allocation results that bear no relationship to actual AI infrastructure consumption.

### Driver Mapping for Financial Services

| Cost Pool | Primary Driver | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Core banking infrastructure | Account volume + transaction count | Core systems serve accounts and process transactions |
| Payment processing infrastructure | Transaction count by type | Processing costs scale with transaction volume |
| AI model inference infrastructure | API calls by product line | Inference costs scale directly with model usage |
| Risk calculation systems | Position count × calculation complexity | Risk systems scale with portfolio complexity |
| Data storage and management | Data volume (GB) by product line | Storage costs scale with data footprint |
| Customer-facing technology | Monthly active users | Customer systems scale with engagement |
| Compliance and reporting systems | Regulatory entity count | Compliance infrastructure scales with regulated products |
| Fraud detection infrastructure | Transaction count | Fraud systems process every transaction |

### Implementation Steps

**Step 1: Instrument AI infrastructure for attribution**

AI inference costs are the fastest-growing and most difficult-to-attribute category in financial services. Instrument your AI systems to capture usage by product line:

```python
# Add attribution metadata to every model inference call
inference_request = {
    'model': 'credit_risk_model_v3',
    'product_line': 'commercial_lending',
    'use_case': 'loan_underwriting',
    'input_tokens': 512,
    'output_tokens': 128,
    'timestamp': '2026-09-01T14:23:00Z'
}

# Track monthly by product line
monthly_inference_costs = (
    inference_logs_df
    .groupby(['product_line', 'model'])
    .agg({'cost_usd': 'sum', 'api_calls': 'count'})
)
```

**Step 2: Build the technology cost attribution model**

```python
fintech_driver_mapping = {
    'core_banking_infrastructure': 'weighted_account_transaction_volume',
    'payment_processing': 'transaction_count_by_type',
    'ai_model_inference': 'api_calls_by_product',
    'risk_calculation_systems': 'position_count_weighted_complexity',
    'data_storage': 'data_volume_gb',
    'customer_facing_tech': 'monthly_active_users',
    'compliance_systems': 'regulatory_entity_count',
    'fraud_detection': 'transaction_count'
}
```

**Step 3: Handle shared AI model costs**

When a single AI model serves multiple product lines, attribute inference costs based on actual usage:

```python
def attribute_shared_model_costs(model_total_cost, usage_by_product):
    """
    Attribute shared AI model costs proportionally to API call volume
    
    Args:
        model_total_cost: Total monthly cost of the AI model
        usage_by_product: Dict of {product_line: api_call_count}
    
    Returns:
        Dict of {product_line: allocated_cost}
    """
    total_calls = sum(usage_by_product.values())
    
    return {
        product: (calls / total_calls) * model_total_cost
        for product, calls in usage_by_product.items()
    }
```

### SOX and Regulatory Considerations

Financial services technology cost attribution may be subject to SOX controls. Ensure your implementation:
- Maintains a complete audit trail of all allocation decisions
- Documents methodology changes with effective dates
- Preserves prior period allocations for comparison
- Has appropriate controls over driver data inputs

### Expected Impact

Financial services firms implementing usage-based attribution typically find:
- High-transaction-volume products (payments, retail banking) receiving higher infrastructure allocations
- Low-volume, high-value products (wealth management, commercial banking) receiving lower allocations than under revenue-percentage methods
- AI infrastructure costs attributed to the products actually generating AI usage — often revealing that certain products have significantly higher AI cost-to-revenue ratios than previously understood

## Cross-Industry Observations

Having applied this methodology across technology, pharmaceutical manufacturing, healthcare, and financial services environments, several patterns emerge consistently:

**The concentration problem is universal**
In every industry, proxy-based allocation concentrates shared costs in the largest product line or business unit — because the proxies (headcount, revenue, bed count) tend to correlate with overall scale rather than actual resource consumption. Usage-based attribution consistently corrects this concentration.

**The magnitude of correction is often surprising**
Organizations expect modest adjustments. In practice, the correction in individual product line allocations often exceeds 30-50%, and in some cases is an order of magnitude larger. The gap between proxy allocation and usage-based allocation is a measure of how far the legacy methodology had drifted from operational reality.

**Stakeholder resistance is proportional to the impact**
Product lines that benefit from the correction — those that were overallocated under the proxy method — typically support the change. Product lines that face increased allocations under usage-based attribution — those that were underallocated — often resist it. Prepare clear, evidence-based explanations of why the new allocation is more accurate.

**Data availability improves over time**
The first implementation often uses proxy drivers in some cost pools because consumption data is not yet available. As organizations build usage monitoring into their operational systems, more cost pools can migrate from proxy to consumption-based drivers. This is expected — implement with what you have and improve over time.
